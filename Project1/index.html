Project 1: Pre-trained Vision and Vision–Language Models

Haopeng Chen · October 2026 · Graduate track (Parts 1, 2 and 3)

Abstract

I study two frozen pre-trained models on a small set of COCO photos. With torchvision's ViT-B/16 (ImageNet-1k weights) I show how a 224×224 image becomes 196 patch tokens plus one [CLS] token, check that the learned patch projection is a single linear map applied to every patch, and look at what the projection filters and positional embeddings learned. On 20 images from five ImageNet categories the classifier reaches a strict top-1 accuracy of 17/20 (0.85) and 19/20 (0.95) when "notebook" counts as "laptop", a rule fixed before the run. Two of the three strict errors are laptops called "notebook", and the third is an upside-down umbrella called "mountain tent".

With OpenAI CLIP ViT-B/32 I re-implement the image–text scoring (normalise, cosine, scale by eτ = 100, softmax) and reproduce CLIP's own logits to within 10−5. All three prompt templates classify the same 20 images correctly; the wording changes only the margins, which grow from 8.80 to 9.77 logits on average for the longer templates. Image embeddings group by category (mean cosine 0.778 within a category, 0.532 across), but background, texture and scene also shape them.

For Part 3 I ask whether CLIP can tell two actions with the same object apart ("riding" vs "pushing" a bicycle) on 12 matched sets of 24 images. CLIP finds the right object for 24/24 images but picks the right action description for only 18/24 (7/12 sets with both images right); its action margins are about six times smaller than its object margins. Cropping each image to the person and the object, with padding so nothing else is cut away, has little effect (19/24 to 21/24 images, mean margin change +0.0008 with a 95% interval that includes zero).

1. Setup and reproducibility

1.1 Models

  • Part 1 uses torchvision's vit_b_16 with weights ViT_B_16_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1 (ViT-Base, 16×16 patches, a supervised ImageNet-1k classifier).
  • Parts 2 and 3 use OpenAI's CLIP ViT-B/32, loaded through Hugging Face transformers from the checkpoint openai/clip-vit-base-patch32.

Both models are used frozen. Nothing is trained or fine-tuned, and both run in evaluation mode without gradients in float32.

1.2 Compute, software and caching

All forward passes ran once on a GPU server with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090. That run saved what the analysis needs: ViT logits and top-5 classes, the ViT patch-projection weights, positional embeddings and class token, CLIP's unnormalised image and text embeddings, its raw logit_scale, and CLIP's own built-in logits for every image–text batch, which serve as the reference for my own computation. The notebooks then load these cached arrays on a laptop CPU and do all similarity math, metrics and plots there, so the embeddings are computed once and reused. Table 1 lists both machines.

1.3 Preprocessing

  • ViT-B/16 uses the checkpoint's own transforms: resize the shorter side to 256 (bilinear), center-crop 224×224, scale to [0, 1], normalise with the ImageNet mean (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and std (0.229, 0.224, 0.225).
  • CLIP uses its processor for openai/clip-vit-base-patch32: resize the shorter side to 224 (bicubic), center-crop 224×224, scale to [0, 1], normalise with the CLIP mean (0.4815, 0.4578, 0.4082) and std (0.2686, 0.2613, 0.2758). Texts go through CLIP's BPE tokenizer and are padded to the longest prompt in the batch.

Both center crops drop the parts of a wide or tall photo that fall outside the central square. This matters in Part 3 (Section 4.3).

1.4 Images

All 44 images come from COCO 2017 (train2017 and val2017). Each COCO image is a Flickr photo that its owner released under a Creative Commons license, and COCO records that license and the original Flickr URL. I use only licenses that allow derivatives, because the figures show resized and cropped versions. Parts 1 and 2 use CC BY and CC BY-SA photos only. Part 3 uses CC BY and CC BY-SA photos plus one CC BY-NC-SA photo (set01b), taken because no better-licensed candidate showed the action clearly. The images are used for non-commercial coursework only, with attribution. The appendix lists every file with its COCO id and split, license link and Flickr link.

For Parts 1 and 2 I picked 4 images in each of five categories that exist in ImageNet: zebra, banana, pizza, laptop and umbrella. In every image the target object is the main object. Three images per category are clear views; the fourth was chosen to be harder: a zebra behind a chain-link fence, a half-peeled banana, a close-up of a pizza slice being pulled away and cut off by the border, a laptop lid seen from behind and covered in stickers, and an umbrella lying upside down in tall grass. The selection rules for Part 3 are in Section 4.1.

2. Part 1: Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16)

2.1 Task 1: image patches and model inputs

Cutting the image into patches

I wrote a function that cuts an image into non-overlapping 16×16 tiles in row-major order, so patch k sits at grid row ⌊k/14⌋ and column k mod 14. Figure 1 applies it to the 224×224 model input of one zebra photo.

A 224×224 image with 16×16 patches gives a 14×14 grid, so there are 196 patches. Each patch holds 3·16·16 = 768 raw values. ViT puts one learned [CLS] token in front, so the encoder sees a sequence of 197 tokens. The checkpoint agrees: its positional embedding has 197 rows, and its patch projection has 768 output channels with a 16×16 kernel and stride 16.

What the three input components do

  • Patch projection. A transformer works on a sequence of vectors. The projection turns each 16×16×3 patch into one 768-dimensional token with a learned linear map, zk = W·vec(patchk) + b. torchvision writes this as a convolution whose kernel size and stride are both 16, which applies the same W to every patch.
  • Positional embeddings. Self-attention has no notion of order: shuffle the patches and the outputs are shuffled the same way. ViT therefore adds a learned vector to each token that depends only on its position in the sequence (197 vectors). Nothing tells the model that positions form a 2-D grid; it has to learn that.
  • [CLS] token. One extra learned vector, not taken from any patch, is placed first. Over 12 attention layers it gathers information from all patches, and the classification head reads only the final [CLS] vector to produce the 1000 ImageNet logits. It is the model's summary of the whole image.

The input to the first encoder layer is therefore [cls; z0; …; z195] + pos_embedding, a 197×768 matrix.

My patches are not what the model sees

The tiles in Figure 1 are raw pixels. The model first normalises the image and then multiplies each flattened patch by the learned 768×768 matrix W (the 768×3×16×16 convolution kernel, reshaped) and adds a bias. To check this, I cut the normalised input into 196 patches with my own function, computed the 196 tokens as one matrix product, and compared them with the output of the convolution, reshaped the way torchvision flattens its 14×14 grid. In float64 the largest absolute difference was 1.1 × 10−16. So the patch embedding is one fixed linear map, and token k of the sequence is exactly patch k in row-major order. My patch picture shows what goes into that map; the model only ever works with its 768 outputs per patch.

Each row of W is a 3×16×16 filter and can be drawn as a small RGB image. Following the ViT paper (Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021, Fig. 7), Figure 2 shows the leading principal components of the 768 filters. They are smooth, low-frequency patterns with opposing colours: horizontal green/magenta bands, a left/right split, blue against orange; later components add finer checkerboard detail. The first 28 components explain only 41.7% of the variance (PC 1 alone 2.7%), so the filters spread over many directions. Individual filters are much harder to read, and many look like noise.

Positional embeddings learned a 2-D layout

For each patch position I computed the cosine similarity between its positional embedding and those of all 196 positions and drew the result on the 14×14 grid (Figure 3). Every map peaks at the query. Direct and diagonal neighbours have a mean similarity of 0.61, positions 1.5 to 3 patches away 0.14, and positions 6 to 10 patches away −0.15. The maps also show a cross through the query: same column 0.54, same row 0.37, neither −0.07. Similarity does not fall smoothly with distance (Pearson r = −0.32 over all pairs), and border positions resemble each other. The model was given a 1-D list of 197 vectors and recovered row and column structure from data alone, as the ViT paper reports for its larger models.

Successful examples

  1. Zebra behind a chain-link fence (371004), zebra 0.896. The wire crosses almost every patch, but the stripe texture still wins. The model registers the fence: "chainlink fence" and "worm fence" appear in the top-5 at 0.001 or less.
  2. Half-peeled banana (99642), banana 0.867. Only part of the fruit is visible, on a dark surface. The yellow peel and curved shape are enough; the small alternatives (spatula 0.013, the mushroom bolete 0.007) pick up the flat, pale flesh.
  3. Sticker-covered laptop lid (417153), laptop 0.990. I expected this view from behind to be hard. It is the most confident prediction of all 20. A lid seen from behind with a corner of keyboard showing is a common way laptops appear in photos, so my idea of "hard" did not match the model's.
  4. Cat under an umbrella (286141), umbrella 0.980. The cat sits in the middle of the crop, but the open umbrella fills most of the frame. Cat classes reach the top-5 (tiger cat, tabby) with about 0.001 each.

Incorrect or uncertain examples

  1. Netbook with a mouse (248631), notebook 0.402, wrong under the strict rule. The probability splits three ways: notebook 0.402, mouse 0.270, laptop 0.136. Part of the split is the laptop/notebook overlap. The rest comes from a second object, the mouse, competing for the single label. This is the least confident prediction in the set.
  2. Chromebook (8999), notebook 0.708, wrong under the strict rule. There is one device and nothing else, yet ImageNet has two names for it. The model prefers "notebook" (0.708) over "laptop" (0.114). It recognises the object, and the strict rule rejects only the name.
  3. Upside-down orange umbrella in tall grass (565572), mountain tent 0.517, wrong under both rules. The canopy lies open side up, so the usual dome-over-a-handle shape is gone. What remains is bright nylon in a grassy outdoor scene, which is typical of tent photos. Umbrella still gets 0.322, so the model is torn. The correct black umbrella on snow (319322, umbrella 0.813) also has "mountain tent" as its runner-up (0.022).
  4. MacBook next to a beer (232121), laptop 0.556, correct but uncertain. A glass of beer at the edge of the crop gets 0.187. With one label per image, two visible objects share the probability.

What a fixed set of 1000 categories cannot answer

  • The model always answers, and only from its list. The softmax spreads exactly 1.0 over the same 1000 classes for every image. There is no "none of these", which is why our five-way task needs an "other" column.
  • Overlapping labels make "correct" our decision. On the four laptop photos the model divides probability between "laptop" and "notebook" (for 248631, 0.136 + 0.402). Whether "notebook" counts is not part of the model. Strict (0.85) and lenient (0.95) accuracy differ only because of this one overlap.
  • One label per image, even when several things are visible. Umbrella images 286141 and 123916 each show a cat clearly. ImageNet has five cat classes, but together they get only 0.0025 and 0.0055. A top-1 label cannot say "a cat and an umbrella".
  • No relations, actions or attributes. "What is the cat doing under the umbrella?", "Is the umbrella upside down?" and "How many bananas are there?" have no answer in the output space.
  • Concepts outside the list are forced into the nearest class. There is no class for person, snow or grass. The upside-down umbrella became a "mountain tent". Adding a category means training a new head on labelled examples; the class names are just labels on 1000 outputs, so a new class cannot be described in words. CLIP in Part 2 removes this limit.

3. Part 2: CLIP ViT-B/32

Part 2 uses the same 20 images as Part 1. The model weights stay fixed throughout.

3.1 Task 1: implementing the image–text similarity

CLIP's two encoders map an image to a vector x and a text to a vector t in a shared 512-dimensional space. The cached embeddings are not unit length (for example, the first three images have norms of 10.7 to 11.1 and one set of five prompts has norms of 8.4 to 10.0), so normalisation is a real step. I implemented the scoring in numpy:

  1. L2-normalise every embedding: x̂ = x / ‖x‖2, and the same for t.
  2. Cosine similarity between image i and text j:
    sij = x̂i⊤t̂j ∈ [−1, 1],   or for all pairs at once S = X̂T̂⊤.
  3. Logits with the learned scale. The checkpoint stores the logarithm τ = 4.6052 of the multiplier, so
    zij = eτ · sij,   eτ = 100.00.
    (CLIP clips the multiplier at 100 during training, and this checkpoint sits at that limit.)
  4. Softmax over the candidate texts for each image:
    pij = exp(zij) / Σk exp(zik).
  5. Prediction: ŷi = argmaxj zij, which is the same as the argmax of sij or pij.

I compared my logits with logits_per_image from a full CLIP forward pass on the GPU. The largest absolute differences were 5.6 × 10−6, 8.5 × 10−6 and 9.7 × 10−6 for the three templates of Task 2, on logits of size 15 to 35. That is float32 rounding, every template passes numpy's allclose test with default tolerances, and the predicted class is identical everywhere.

Cosine similarity, logits and probabilities

Cosine similarity belongs to one image–text pair and does not depend on the other candidates. In Figure 7 all cosines fall between about 0.14 and 0.33, and with "a photo of a {c}" the correct prompt leads the best wrong prompt by only 0.06 to 0.13. Image–text cosines in CLIP are small in absolute terms; only their ranking carries meaning. Logits are the same numbers times 100, so the ranking and colour pattern are unchanged and a cosine gap of 0.06 becomes a logit gap of 6. The softmax normalises each row over the five candidates. With gaps of 6 or more (e6 ≈ 400) almost every row is one-hot, so the probability says little about confidence here. The logit margin keeps more information. The least saturated rows are the hard images: the upside-down umbrella (P = 0.997) and the half-peeled banana (P = 0.998).

Why the softmax depends on the candidate texts

For the three images with the smallest margin I recomputed the softmax after removing either the runner-up prompt or the true prompt (Table 6). The cosines do not change, because removing one prompt cannot change the angle between an image and another prompt. The probabilities do change, because the softmax divides by a sum over whichever candidates are present. Removing the runner-up barely matters, since it held a fraction of a percent. Removing the true class pushes the mass onto wrong prompts: the upside-down umbrella becomes "laptop" with 0.62, although its cosine with "a photo of a laptop" is still 0.191. A softmax probability is a score relative to the offered list, and zero-shot CLIP always picks one of the offered labels even when none fits.

This fits the CLIP paper's remark (Radford et al., 2021) that a single word is an unusual caption, while a short sentence is closer to the alt-text CLIP was trained on.

Prompting is not fine-tuning

Changing the prompt changes only where the text encoder places the five class queries. "zebra" and "a photo of a zebra" are related but different vectors: across categories and template pairs their cosine is between 0.909 and 0.963. The image embeddings, the weights of both encoders and the logit scale stay fixed. No loss is computed and no parameter is updated, so a prompt can be swapped per query at no cost and is fully reversible. Fine-tuning computes a loss on labelled examples and updates the weights by gradient descent. That changes the encoders themselves, moves the image embeddings too, and applies to every later input. A prompt can only re-rank what the frozen embedding space already encodes; it cannot teach CLIP a visual distinction it does not already make.

This does not show that either model is better in general. ViT-B/16 is a supervised 1000-way classifier trained with cross-entropy on ImageNet-1k (about 1.28 M labelled images); CLIP is trained contrastively on about 400 M web image–text pairs, and its "classes" are prompts chosen at test time. ViT picks one of 1000 classes, some of them near-synonyms (laptop/notebook) or visually close (umbrella/tent), while CLIP here picks one of 5 well-separated prompts. The patch sizes (16 vs 32) and preprocessing differ. With 20 hand-picked images, one image is 5 percentage points, so gaps of one to three images support no general claim.

4. Part 3: telling actions apart when the object is the same

Parts 1 and 2 asked what the main object is. Part 3 asks whether CLIP ViT-B/32 can tell what a person is doing with an object: riding vs pushing a bicycle, flying vs holding a kite, sitting vs lying on a bench. The model, the scoring and the logit scale are those of Part 2.

4.1 Task 1: hypothesis and comparison sets

Hypothesis (written down before any result)

CLIP's image–text similarity is dominated by which objects and scene are present, not by what the person does with the object. When two descriptions name the same object and differ only in the action, CLIP will match images to descriptions much less reliably than it recognises the object. Three checkable predictions follow:

  • P1 (2×2 test). Image-to-description accuracy over the 24 images will be above chance (50%) but clearly below perfect, with small margins.
  • P2 (object vs action). Against all 24 descriptions, an image's correct object will be found more often, and with larger margins, than its correct action.
  • P3 (text side). The two descriptions of a set will have very similar text embeddings, more similar than descriptions that share the verb but name a different object.

Selection rules

The rules were fixed before any CLIP output existed.

  1. Each set fixes one COCO object category and two actions, side a and side b. Descriptions follow one template, "a person ⟨verb⟩ a ⟨object⟩". Two sets add words to side a ("riding a surfboard on a wave", "skiing on skis").
  2. Every COCO image has about five human captions. A caption votes for a side when it matches the verb phrase followed by the object noun (with up to three words between). An image is a candidate for a side only if at least two captions vote for that side and none for the other.
  3. The image must contain an annotated, non-crowd instance of the object and an annotated person. The object box is the largest instance; the person box is the person closest to it among people at least 25% as large as the largest person.
  4. NoDerivs licenses are excluded because crops are shown. Attribution and ShareAlike licenses come first; a NonCommercial license was used only once, for set01b.
  5. Candidates are ranked by license, then by having exactly one person, then by caption votes and person size. I checked the top candidates by eye on contact sheets with their boxes and picked one image per side where the action is visible and the person and object are not tiny or hidden.

This gives 12 sets and 24 images, each set with two images and two descriptions. Image a matches text a and image b matches text b (Table 12, Figure 13).

Known confounds

The two images of a set are different photos, so more than the action changes:

  • Scene. Side-a actions often come with a typical scene (a wave, a snow slope, open sky), while side-b photos are more often on a beach, a street or a parking lot. CLIP may match scene words instead of pose. The two set12 scenes differ a lot: a close-up face eating against a boy at a cutting board.
  • Ambiguous poses. In set04a the "rider" stands almost still on the board and talks on the phone. Set09 (throwing vs catching a frisbee) differs only in pose and frisbee position.
  • Object size and visibility. Some objects are small (the set08a kite, the set09 frisbees, the set04a skateboard). CLIP's center crop also hides part of the photo: on the full images only 53% of the pizza box reaches the input for set12b, 64% of the kite (set08a) and the bench (set11b), and 71% of the skateboard (set04b).
  • Text beyond the verb. "on a wave" and "on skis" add words to side a, and "standing next to" (set02, set03, set10) is longer than "riding".
  • Resolution and selection. COCO photos are about 640 px on the long side and CLIP sees 224×224, so hands and small objects shrink to a few pixels. Caption matching and the visual check favour clear, typical scenes, which makes the task easier than a random sample.

P1 holds. On full images 18 of 24 images (0.75) pick their own description, and 7 of 12 sets have both images right. If CLIP were guessing, 18 or more of 24 would happen with probability 0.011, so CLIP carries some action information. The signal is weak. The median margin is 0.008 in cosine units, on top of image–text cosines that all lie between about 0.27 and 0.36. At CLIP's scale of 100, a margin of 0.01 is a softmax of about 0.73 against 0.27, and the mean probability of the correct description is only 0.675. The failures are set02a (riding a horse), set04b (carrying a skateboard), set07b (carrying a snowboard), set08b (holding a kite) and both images of set09 (throwing vs catching a frisbee). In set04, set07 and set08 both images prefer the side-a activity, which looks like a bias toward the more typical, more photographed action; set02 goes the other way, and the set09 images swap descriptions. Matching in the other direction, description to image, is lower still (16/24 texts, 4/12 sets), partly because some photos score higher with every description (set09a has 0.35 with both).

P2 holds. Against all 24 descriptions, every image ranks a description of its own object first (24/24). The median object margin (0.047) is about six times the median action margin (0.008), and the object margin is larger for 23 of 24 images. P3 holds, with one limit. The two descriptions of a set have cosines from 0.862 to 0.975 (mean 0.927). That is higher on average than pairs sharing the verb but not the object (mean 0.846) and much higher than pairs sharing neither (0.733). For 18 of 24 descriptions the nearest other description is the set partner. In the six exceptions the nearest description names a related object, and in five of them it also shares the verb, for example "riding a horse" → "riding a bicycle" and "carrying skis" → "carrying a snowboard"; the sixth is "skiing on skis" → "riding a snowboard". The text encoder does encode the verb, but the object word dominates, and the ranges overlap (lowest within-set 0.862, highest cross-set 0.937).

This explains why the margins are so small. For unit vectors the margin v̂·(t̂own − t̂other) can be at most ‖t̂own − t̂other‖ = √(2(1 − c)), where c is the text–text cosine; for these pairs that bound is 0.22 to 0.53. The observed margins use a median of only about 3% of it, so the image embedding is almost orthogonal to the "action direction". Sets whose descriptions are more alike tend to have smaller margins (Pearson r = −0.43 over 12 sets, a weak tendency with 12 points).

The most confident successes are set12 (eating vs cutting a pizza, margins +0.017 and +0.021), set06 (skiing vs carrying skis, +0.014 and +0.021) and set03 (riding vs standing next to a motorcycle, +0.013 and +0.015). In set12 the two scenes differ a lot (a face close-up against a cutting board), and set06 and set03 each have extra words in one description ("skiing on skis", "standing next to"). Set02b, "standing next to a horse", has the largest margin of all (+0.032), and its description is the longer one. Part of these successes may therefore come from scene and wording. Among the failures, set02a ("riding a horse", P(own) = 0.36) loses although the rider is plainly on the horse. Set04 and set08 fail on side b: the man holding a skateboard upright and the boy holding a kite are both scored closer to "riding" and "flying". In set04 the rider barely moves, so the action is ambiguous even for a person.

4.3 Task 3: one controlled change, removing the context

Hypothesis and design (stated before the crop results)

CLIP encodes the whole input into one vector, and the person and object often fill only part of the photo. The background (street, beach, sky, crowd) might dilute or mislead the action signal. If so, removing most of the background should raise the margins, the image-to-description accuracy and the number of sets with both images correct. If the background helps instead, for example a wave for surfing or a slope for skiing, the crop should hurt the images whose action comes with such a scene.

The one change: each image is replaced by its crop to the union of the COCO person and object boxes, enlarged by 10% of the box size on each side and clamped to the image. The 24 images, the 24 descriptions, the model and the scoring stay the same.

First attempt, and why it was confounded

My first version passed the crops straight to CLIP's processor. With standard preprocessing, accuracy rose from 18/24 to 21/24 and both-correct sets from 7/12 to 9/12 (five images wrong → right, two right → wrong; exact McNemar p = 0.45; mean margin change +0.0013, bootstrap 95% interval [−0.0027, +0.0052]). Most of this pattern came from geometry. The processor center-crops the resized image, and many crops are tall, so it cut away their top and bottom. For five crops (set04a, set07a, set08a, set11b, set12b) less than half of the object box reached the input, and for three of them none of it did: the skateboard and snowboard under the feet and the kite in the sky (Figure 18). These five images lost margin on average (−0.0057) while the other 19 gained (+0.0032), and the change in visible object share correlated with the margin change (r = +0.38). Set07a, one of the two new failures, is one whose snowboard was cut out. That version changed context and object visibility at once.

Main comparison: padded full image vs padded crop

To remove the visibility change, both the full image and the crop are padded to a square with a grey close to CLIP's mean colour (RGB 122, 116, 104) before the processor. The processor then only resizes and cuts nothing away, so the person and object boxes reach the input completely in both conditions (Figure 18, padded columns). Two other differences remain. The crop shows the person 1.0 to 2.05 times larger after resizing, so cropping removes context and zooms in at the same time. And set06b's crop is the whole image, so its two conditions are identical.

Result: removing the context has little effect. With padding in both conditions, accuracy goes from 19/24 to 21/24 and both-correct sets from 7/12 to 9/12. Two images flip from wrong to right (set03a riding a motorcycle, set09b catching a frisbee) and none flip back; with two flips an exact McNemar test gives p = 0.50. The mean margin change is +0.0008, with a 95% bootstrap interval over sets of [−0.0007, +0.0024] (10,000 resamples, both images of a set resampled together). Margins go up for 14 images and down for 9 (sign test p = 0.40, set06b a tie), and per set 8 go up and 4 down (p = 0.39). The direction agrees with the dilution idea, but the effect is about a tenth of a typical margin and cannot be told from zero with 24 images. Object recognition is perfect in both conditions. Set02a, set04b and set06a are wrong with and without context.

Padding also changes the story of the first attempt. With standard preprocessing the crop seemed to help the "passive" side-b images (10 up, 1 down) and hurt the sport images on side a. In the padded comparison the split is gone (side a 6 up and 6 down; side b 8 up and 3 down, p = 0.23; both exploratory, since the split was chosen after Task 2). Set07a (snowboarding) is right in both padded conditions, so its failure in the first attempt came from the cut-out snowboard. Set06a (skiing) is wrong even on the padded full image, so removing the slope did not cause its failure.

Padding alone moves the results about as much as cropping. Going from the standard to the padded full image changes no content, only the scale and the grey bars, yet it flips 5 images (set07b, set08b and set09a to right; set03a and set06a to wrong; McNemar p = 1.0) and changes margins by a median of 0.0049, about 60% of the median margin of 0.0082, with a mean change of zero. With margins this small, harmless preprocessing choices decide several images either way, which is more evidence that the action signal is weak.

Alternative explanations and limits

  • Zoom is still tied to context. The margin change shows no clear relation to the zoom factor (r = −0.11) or to the share of the image the crop keeps (r = +0.19), but 24 images say little. Separating the two would need a different change, such as greying out everything outside the union box without cropping.
  • Context is evidence as well as noise. A wave, a slope or a road can support an action, and the crop removes it along with the distractors. A near-zero average may hide images that gain and images that lose for these opposite reasons.
  • Padding changes the input distribution, since CLIP rarely saw grey bars in training. Both padded conditions share this, so it should not bias their comparison, but it is why Task 2 uses the standard full images.
  • Sample size and selection. 12 sets and 24 images give every test here low power, and the COCO caption search plus the visual check favour clear, typical scenes, so effect sizes may not carry over to other photos.

5. Conclusions

  • ViT-B/16 turns a 224×224 image into 196 patch tokens plus a [CLS] token through one linear projection, and its learned positional embeddings recover the 2-D grid. As a classifier it reaches 17/20 (strict) and 19/20 (lenient) on our images. Its errors come mostly from the fixed label set: overlapping names, one label per image, and no way to express concepts outside the 1000 classes.
  • CLIP's scoring is normalise, cosine, multiply by 100, softmax, and my implementation reproduces CLIP's logits to float32 rounding. Softmax probabilities are relative to the candidate list. Prompt wording left accuracy at 20/20 but changed the margins, and it does not touch the model's weights.
  • CLIP's image embeddings group by category first and also reflect scene, background and texture.
  • CLIP recognises the object in 24/24 Part 3 images but separates two actions on that object in only 18/24 images (7/12 sets), with margins about six times smaller than its object margins. Removing the background, without cutting away the person or object, has little effect on this, and preprocessing details alone flip several decisions.
